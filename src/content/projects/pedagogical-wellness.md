# Pedagogical Wellness: A Community of Practice as Research Site

*Reframing educator well-being as a design problem — and finding that the change it produces is not where the field has been looking for it.*

## Context

Faculty wellness in higher education is most often treated as a personal responsibility: another wellness webinar, another mindfulness app, another reminder to take a walk. That framing is well-intentioned and, in practice, largely inert.

It leaves faculty doing more — adding wellness to an already overflowing list — and it leaves the structures that generate the strain untouched.

Health sciences educators carry a particular version of this load. They teach across clinical, classroom, and online contexts; they translate rapidly evolving science into curriculum; and they hold the emotional weight of preparing students for work in human suffering. The standard institutional response — episodic wellness programming — does not match the structural nature of the problem.

## Challenge

The challenge was to design a faculty development model that took pedagogical wellness seriously as a teaching and learning question rather than a self-care question — and then to study it rigorously enough that the findings would transfer beyond one institution.

That meant three things at once:

- **Reframing wellness as design.** Position well-being as something instructional design choices help cause or prevent — pacing, workload, feedback structures, community — rather than something faculty fix on their own time.
- **Building, not broadcasting.** Create a sustained Community of Practice with shared inquiry, not a one-off workshop series.
- **Producing research, not a program report.** Treat the CoP as a research site with an IRB-aligned design, explicit theory of change, and analysis capable of surfacing findings that contradict the program's own assumptions.

That third commitment turned out to matter most. The study's central finding is a correction to how educational development measures its own impact.

## Approach

With Early Researcher Grant funding from the POD Network, I designed and launched a Pedagogical Wellness Community of Practice that operates simultaneously as a faculty development structure and as the site of a qualitative study.

**The community.** Thirty-four faculty and staff enrolled across all colleges and multiple administrative units, with approximately twelve participating in each of eight monthly sessions from September 2025 through April 2026. The arc moved deliberately from relationship to structure — beginning with trust, connections, and commitments; moving through wellness-centered course design and trauma-informed practice; and closing on institutional structures and cultural advocacy.

**The design choices that mattered:**

- **Anchored inside the SoTL Collective.** The CoP sits within existing scholarship infrastructure, so participants inherit IRB-aligned scaffolding, shared methods, and a dissemination pipeline rather than building each from scratch.
- **Faculty and staff together.** Staff were recruited as full members, not as support. This proved consequential: much of the study's evidence of changed conduct is administrative rather than instructional, and a faculty-only cohort would not have surfaced it.
- **Norms before content.** Members wrote their own ground rules in Month 2, before the substantive curriculum began.
- **Rotated facilitation.** Distributing facilitation kept the community from becoming one person's program.
- **Generative AI governed openly.** The documentation workflow, including AI notetaking, was adopted by member vote rather than imposed — modeling the transparency the work argues for.

**The study.** The research design triangulated three sources: structured facilitator memos completed after each monthly session; semi-structured exit interviews with five active participants; and reflexive memos from the research team. Three researchers coded using overlapping-pair assignment against a shared codebook of twenty starter codes, following reflexive thematic analysis. The study was reviewed as exempt (IRB #2025-082) and closed out in October 2026.

The analysis was organized around one question — how does participation in a Community of Practice influence faculty and staff perceptions and practices related to pedagogical wellness? — with three sub-questions that proved more useful than the main one: *What changes through sustained participation? Where does that change appear? How far can it travel?*

## Findings

Analysis yielded five interrelated themes, ordered from what changed to where change met its limits.

| Theme | Finding |
|---|---|
| **Empathy through storytelling** | "Not just a person who sits in an office." Sustained cross-unit contact produced recognition of colleagues as whole professionals, which preceded any change in practice. |
| **Shared language as legitimation** | "A name for what I was already doing." For participants already enacting wellness-oriented practice, the CoP's contribution was a name for it — converting tacit, privately improvised work into something visible and defensible to others. |
| **Levels of change** | "It deepened, it didn't change." Change registered along a gradient from perception to conduct, and most participants located themselves nearer the perception end without treating that as a lesser outcome. |
| **Psychological safety and localized agency** | "A safe space to start the conversation." Participants acted where they had standing — their own courses, their own units — and described the boundary of that standing precisely. |
| **Wellness as a structural condition** | "Who's checking on us?" The people carrying wellness work, educational developers included, described their own wellness as institutionally unmet. |

### The argument this produces

The most useful result is methodological, and it is uncomfortable for the field.

**If you measure changed teaching practice, you will systematically under-detect this kind of intervention.** The change a Community of Practice produces appears first as vocabulary uptake, then as shifts in attention, then — where participants hold authority — as administrative conduct. A program evaluation that looks only for new instructional techniques will conclude that very little happened, and will be wrong.

This reframes the evaluative question itself: not *did participants change their teaching*, but *what changed, where did it change, and how far could it travel*. It also reframes the work's category. A teaching community that surfaces institutional levers is doing organizational development, not only faculty development.

The Menges Award matters for a specific reason: POD explicitly excludes program-implementation accounts and best-practice reflections from consideration. The award recognizes research. That distinction is the one I would most want a reader to take from this case.

## My role

As Principal Investigator and lead designer, I authored the funded proposal, designed the Community of Practice and the study in parallel, secured IRB review, facilitated the monthly sessions, conducted exit interviews, served as one of three coders, and am first author on the resulting work.

The work draws on instructional design, qualitative research, faculty development, and project management. It is also shaped by my own experience as a working parent and scholar-practitioner balancing leadership with caregiving — which is why the question of sustainable scholarship has never been theoretical for me.

## What I learned, and what's next

**Framing does more work than curriculum.** Participants engaged differently once wellness was positioned as a shared design problem rather than a personal deficit. The single most-cited contribution was not a technique but a vocabulary.

**The limits participants name are more credible than the limits researchers assert.** One participant described the reach of the work precisely: it ripples through the first layer, the part we directly influence, and not reliably past it. That ceiling, stated by a participant rather than claimed by the research team, is what keeps the findings honest.

**The developers are inside the problem.** The people designing wellness interventions reported their own wellness as unaddressed. Any serious account of pedagogical wellness has to include the educational developers carrying it.

**Next:** Year Two of the Community of Practice is running now, shifting from shared inquiry toward member-led pilot projects. The manuscript is in preparation, and the facilitation materials and codebook are being prepared for open release so that peer institutions can adapt both the community design and the measurement approach.
